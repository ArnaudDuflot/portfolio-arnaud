## The friction

When I joined HelloBible, user feedback lived in five different inboxes: support email, Zendesk tickets, a WhatsApp beta group of 200+ engaged testers, the App Store reviews, and the Play Store reviews. The product team had no unified view - they were getting feedback in fragments, often days late, and the most loyal beta testers (the WhatsApp crew) were essentially shouting into a void.

The result? Sprint planning was driven by whichever feedback channel happened to be loudest that week, not by signal density.

## My approach

I treated each channel as a data source with a different shape, and built a normalization layer that mapped everything to the same schema: `{source, raw_text, category, urgency, user_id, timestamp}`.

The pipeline:

1. **Ingestion** - Zendesk hooks for tickets, manual exports for stores, a WhatsApp-to-sheet bridge for the beta group.
2. **Categorization** - Claude API tags each item (bug / feature request / praise / confusion / churn signal) and extracts the feature area mentioned.
3. **Deduplication** - variants of the same request get clustered.
4. **Visualization** - Google Sheets dashboard the product team consults every Monday before sprint planning.

The orchestration is **n8n** - chosen specifically because the team can read it. n8n's visual flow means anyone on the team can debug or extend the pipeline without me being the single point of failure.

## The result

The product team now opens **one** dashboard before every sprint. Backlog grooming dropped from a 3-hour meeting to a 45-minute one. The WhatsApp beta group went from "shouting into a void" to "directly visible in sprint priorities" - which had a non-trivial impact on beta retention.

## What I learned

The most important design decision was **picking n8n over custom code**. A custom Python pipeline would have been "cleaner" - but I'd have been the only person who could maintain it. n8n trades elegance for distributed ownership, and that's almost always the right trade in a small team.
